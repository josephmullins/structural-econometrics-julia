What Can Models Do? A Guide for Policy Practitioners

Joseph Mullins

The Course So Far

  • You’ve learned about evidence-based decision making
  • You know why we need rigorous evidence: intuition and anecdotes aren’t enough
  • You’ve seen how randomized experiments and quasi-experiments help us measure the effects of policies

Today’s question: What happens when the policy you’re considering hasn’t been tried before?

What Experiments Tell Us

Experiments are the gold standard for answering:

“What was the effect of this specific policy on this specific population?”

That’s incredibly valuable. But policymakers usually face a different question…

What Policymakers Actually Need

“What would happen if we implemented this new policy on our population?”

  • The policy you’re designing may not match any past experiment exactly
  • Your population may differ from the one that was studied
  • You may want to compare several options that have never been tried
  • You may wonder about the willingess to pay for certain policies

This is the gap that economic models fill.

A Simple Example: The Market for Bananas

Three Policies Under Consideration

The government is considering three policies to promote banana consumption:

  1. A 10% demand subsidy — consumers pay 10% less
  2. A 20% demand subsidy — consumers pay 20% less
  3. A 15% supply-side subsidy — producers’ receive 15% more

Two questions:

  • What does each policy do to the price and quantity of bananas?
  • How much are consumers willing to pay for each one?

One solution: run an Experiment

  • Suppose we run an RCT for Policy 1: \[q_{m} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 Z_{m} + \epsilon_{m}\] \[p_{m} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 Z_{m} + \eta_{m}\] where \(Z_{m}\in\{0,1\}\) indicates treatment and \((p,q)\) are (log) price and quantity.
  • This gives us clean estimates of the % effect of subsidy on prices (\(\alpha_1\)) and quantities (\(\beta_1\)).
  • But do \(\alpha_1\) and \(\beta_1\) tell us anything about Policies 2 and 3? Could we extrapolate with these results?

Looking at the Data

Suppose we look at prices and quantities across all our markets.

Why Is It a Cloud?

Prices and quantities are jointly determined by supply and demand.

Lesson

Sometimes we need a model to make sense of the data and identify deep causal objects of interest.

A Simple Theory

A simple economic model:

  • Consumers choose how many bananas to buy: \(\max_{Q} U(Q,X)\) subject to \(PQ + X \leq Y\)
  • Producers choose how many to supply: \(\max_{Q} PQ - C(Q)\) in a competitive market

The right \(U\) and \(C\) generate linear supply and demand across markets:

\[q_{m}^{D} = \kappa_0 - \kappa_1 p_{m} + \zeta_{m} \qquad \text{(demand)}\] \[q_{m}^{S} = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 p_{m} + \xi_{m} \qquad \text{(supply)}\]

where \(\kappa_1\) and \(\gamma_1\) are the demand and supply elasticities and \((\zeta_m, \xi_m)\) are unobserved shifters. The market clears: \(q_m^D = q_m^S\).

  • This generates the cloud we just saw
  • The elasticities are the deep parameters — they govern how the market responds to any price change

Using the Experiment to Identify the Model

The 10% demand subsidy shifts the demand curve. From the experimental estimates \(\alpha_1\) and \(\beta_1\), we can recover the structural elasticities.

Recovering the Deep Parameters

Recall: \(\alpha_1\) is the effect of the consumer subsidy on log price; \(\beta_1\) is the effect on log quantity.

Producers see a price change of \(\alpha_1\), so the supply elasticity is:

\[\gamma_1 = \frac{\beta_1}{\alpha_1}\]

Consumers see a price change of \(\alpha_1 + \log(0.9)\) (market price moved, plus the 10% subsidy), so the demand elasticity is:

\[\kappa_1 = -\frac{\beta_1}{\alpha_1 + \log(0.9)}\]

With \(\gamma_1\) and \(\kappa_1\), we can forecast the equilibrium effects of any price change — including policies we haven’t tested.

With the elasticities in hand, we can forecast all three policies:

  • Policy 3 shifts a different curve — the experiment alone tells us nothing about it, but the model connects them because both work through known changes to prices
  • We can actually forecast the effects of all policies with any source of variation in supply and demand.

We call this well-articulated variation: when a policy’s effect can be traced through the model without needing new parameters.

Point 2

Lesson

Economic models can jointly interpret many sources of variation for the purposes of policy and prediction.

Revealed Preference and Welfare

  • The experiment tells us quantities changed. But is the subsidy worth what it costs?
  • In estimating the demand curve, we implicitly estimated consumers’ utility \(U\) — how they value bananas relative to everything else.
  • This is revealed preference: assuming people know what they like, their choices will reveal it to us
  • We can calculate each consumer’s willingness to pay for the subsidy
  • We can compare costs to government against benefits to consumers

This lets us evaluate policies without assuming we know what’s good for people better than they do.

Point 3

Lesson

Economic models allow us to make welfare statements using revealed preference.

Three Lessons from Bananas

  1. We needed the model to interpret the data. Prices and quantities alone were a meaningless cloud. The theory of supply and demand identified the deep parameters that govern behavior.

  2. The model let us go beyond the experiment. One experiment gave us enough to forecast three policies — including one on the supply side with no direct experimental analogue.

  3. The model let us measure welfare. Not just “did quantities change?” but “are consumers better off, and by how much?”

Now let’s see this at work in a real policy setting.

The General Idea

Preferences, Constraints, and Policy

  • In the banana example: preferences = consumer utility \(U\); constraints = prices and income; policy = the subsidy, which changes prices
  • Models are especially powerful when policies work through known changes to prices and endowments — well-articulated variation

Applying This to Welfare Policy

  • Individuals choose how much to work and whether to participate based on their preferences, given policy rules and labor market opportunities
  • Welfare policies give transfers (endowments) and shape the returns to work (prices) — well-articulated variation

Welfare Reform: The Application

The Setting

In the 1990s, the US overhauled its welfare system.

  • Before the national reform, different states ran different experiments
  • Each tested a different bundle of rules:
    • Work requirements
    • Time limits on benefits
    • Earnings disregards (how much you can earn before losing benefits)

Each experiment was well-designed and carefully evaluated.

What Each Experiment Told Us

Each experiment answered a narrow question:

“This bundle of rules, applied to this population, had these effects on employment, earnings, and child outcomes.”

For example:

  • Minnesota’s MFIP: generous earnings disregard + mandatory services
  • Connecticut’s Jobs First: strict time limits + generous earnings disregard
  • Florida’s FTP: time limits + modest earnings disregard

Each experiment is like testing one subsidy in one market.

The Policymaker’s Problem

You’re a state official designing a new welfare program.

  • None of these experiments match your proposed rules exactly
  • Your state’s population differs from Minnesota’s or Connecticut’s
  • You want to know about a specific combination of rules

Just as with bananas: the experiment on one policy doesn’t directly tell you about a different policy on a different population.

What the Model Does

The same logic as the banana example, applied to a richer setting:

  • Each welfare experiment shifts the budget constraint in a known way
  • Fitting a model to all the experiments simultaneously recovers the deep parameters: how people trade off income, leisure, time with children, and the costs of working
  • With those parameters, we can forecast the effects of any new combination of rules

Different experiments provide different information (just like the demand and supply subsidies) and the model connects them through a common framework.

An Example From My Research

In Mullins (2026), I did exactly this:

  • Took data from multiple welfare reform experiments across US states
  • Estimated a model of maternal labor supply and children’s development
  • Used it to evaluate new policy designs

Key finding: across sites, time limits were primarily responsible for reductions in welfare dependence, while work requirements were the chief source of employment gains.

That insight was impossible to get from the experiments alone.

Where Models Reach Their Limits

Not every policy fits this framework.

  • If a policy works through a channel the model doesn’t represent — social norms, information, neighborhood effects — you can’t just plug it in
  • You’d need to extend the model or build a different one
  • A key requirement is that the variation you want to study is well-articulated inside the model.

Part of the economist’s job is knowing which questions a given model can and can’t handle.

How Do We Know the Model Is Right?

A Fair Question

You might be thinking:

“This sounds like you’re just making assumptions and running with them.”

That’s a fair concern. Here are some points to keep in mind…

Models Are Disciplined by Data

  • Models are estimated, not assumed — parameters are chosen to match real data
  • They can be tested against evidence not used in estimation: estimate on some experiments, check predictions against others
    • In my welfare reform paper, the model was estimated on a subset of experiments and validated against the rest.

Models Are Transparent About Assumptions

  • Every model involves assumptions — but so does every research design
  • Assumptions are written down explicitly
  • You can ask: “What if this assumption is wrong? How much does it matter?”
  • A model lets you be explicit about how you are getting from existing evidence to a conclusion about future policies.
  • This is more transparent than informal reasoning, where assumptions are implicit.
  • Especially important when policy goal is to improve welfare. How do we know this is what people prefer?

Recap

  1. Models help interpret data: without structure, even simple data can be impossible to read
  2. Models extrapolate and interpolate: especially when policies work through known changes to prices and endowments
  3. Models enable welfare calculations: not just “what changed?” but “are people better off?”
  4. Models are disciplined by data: estimated, testable, and transparent (if done well)
